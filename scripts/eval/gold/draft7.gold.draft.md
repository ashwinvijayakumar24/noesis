# Reference Critique: *Case Studies and the Flipped Classroom*  
**Herreid & Schiller**

---

## Overall Assessment

This manuscript reads as a **position/perspective piece** advocating integration of case studies with the flipped classroom model, rather than as an empirical research article. While it is engaging and practically oriented, it suffers from **major methodological weaknesses, overreliance on anecdotal evidence, limited theoretical grounding, and insufficient critical engagement with the literature**. The evidence base is selective and often based on self-report data rather than rigorous comparative studies.

Below is a structured critique with severity ratings.

---

# MAJOR FINDINGS

---

## 1. Lack of Clear Research Question or Contribution  
**Severity: HIGH**

The manuscript does not articulate a clear research question, hypothesis, or defined scholarly contribution.

> “The flipped classroom shows promise. Now what?”

The paper oscillates between:
- Advocacy
- Informal survey reporting
- Literature summary
- Call to action

It is unclear whether this is:
- A research article  
- A literature review  
- A programmatic manifesto  
- A practitioner guide  

**Reviewer 2 comment:**  
*“The manuscript lacks a clearly articulated contribution to the literature. Is this an empirical study, a review, or an opinion piece?”*

---

## 2. Weak Methodology: The 15,000-Member Survey  
**Severity: HIGH**

The authors state:

> “We recently surveyed the 15,000+ members… Two hundred case teachers reported that they teach in a flipped classroom…”

### Major concerns:
- No response rate reported
- No sampling method described
- No survey instrument included
- No demographics provided
- No statistical analysis
- No IRB or ethics mention
- No description of survey validation

If 200 responded out of 15,000+, that implies a response rate potentially around 1–2%, raising **serious nonresponse bias concerns**.

Additionally, the findings reported are anecdotal bullet points (“they also really like it”) rather than analyzed data.

This section would not meet basic empirical research standards.

---

## 3. Overreliance on Self-Report and Perception Data  
**Severity: HIGH**

Many cited studies rely on:
- Student evaluations
- SALG surveys
- Self-reported satisfaction
- Perceived effectiveness

Example:

> “Student evaluations of the course indicated that the classroom flip had a positive impact…”

Perception ≠ learning gains.

Few studies cited use:
- Randomized controlled trials  
- Validated concept inventories  
- Longitudinal retention measures  
- Controlled quasi-experimental designs  

The manuscript does not distinguish between:
- Affective outcomes
- Behavioral outcomes
- Cognitive outcomes

This conflation weakens the argument substantially.

---

## 4. Missing Controls and Confounds in Cited Studies  
**Severity: HIGH**

Several cited comparisons (e.g., Ruddick, 2012) compare:
- One section (flipped)  
- Another section (lecture)

But the draft does not discuss:

- Instructor effect  
- Cohort differences  
- Assessment equivalence  
- Prior preparation  
- Maturation effects  
- Hawthorne effect  

Example:

> “Results showed that the RI students outperformed…”

Were students randomly assigned? Were instructors identical? Were exams blind-graded?

Without addressing these confounds, the claims are overstated.

---

## 5. Inadequate Theoretical Framework  
**Severity: MEDIUM–HIGH**

The manuscript gestures toward learning theory:

> “learning theory supports the new approaches”

But does not specify:
- Constructivism  
- Cognitive load theory  
- Self-determination theory  
- Retrieval practice literature  
- Distributed practice effects  
- Multimedia learning theory (Mayer)

Notably absent:
- Mayer’s Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning  
- Sweller’s Cognitive Load Theory  
- Freeman et al. (2014) meta-analysis on active learning in STEM  
- Prince (2004) review of active learning  
- Deslauriers et al. (2011) experimental active learning study  

These omissions are substantial given the topic.

---

## 6. Selective and Optimistic Literature Framing  
**Severity: MEDIUM–HIGH**

The literature review emphasizes positive findings:

> “All of this bodes well…”

There is little discussion of:
- Mixed findings in flipped classroom meta-analyses  
- Implementation failures  
- Equity concerns (access to technology)  
- Differential effects across student populations  
- Cognitive overload risks of video-based instruction  

A balanced review should acknowledge:
- Publication bias  
- Heterogeneity of flipped implementations  
- The distinction between “flipped” and “active learning”

---

## 7. Conceptual Ambiguity: What Is the Innovation?  
**Severity: MEDIUM**

The paper does not clearly differentiate:

- Flipped classroom  
- Blended learning  
- Team-based learning  
- Just-in-Time Teaching  
- Hybrid courses  

The authors admit:

> “The flipped classroom is similar to other methods…”

If so, what is novel here?

Is the contribution:
- Integration of flipped model with case studies?
- Advocacy for video-enhanced case preparation?
- Centralized case-video repositories?

This needs sharpening.

# STRUCTURAL PROBLEMS

---

## 8. Weak Abstract (or Missing)  
**Severity: HIGH**

No abstract is provided in the draft.  
A scholarly article requires:

- Background
- Purpose
- Methods
- Results
- Conclusions

Without it, the manuscript lacks framing.

---

## 9. Disorganized Flow  
**Severity: MEDIUM**

The structure feels fragmented:

- Advocacy  
- Survey results  
- Literature review  
- Video podcast section  
- Call for standardization  

Transitions are abrupt.

Example:
The paragraph beginning “is more time to spend with students…” appears truncated and misplaced.

---

**Severity: MEDIUM**

Numerous typographical issues:

- “lipped classroom” (repeated throughout)
- “is they read material”
- “peerreviewed”
- “sec-ond”
- “inal exam”
- “dificult”
- “lexible”
- “irst”

These suggest OCR errors or insufficient proofreading.

# CITATION GAPS

---

## 11. Missing Seminal Work in Active Learning  
**Severity: HIGH**

Absent foundational references:

- Freeman et al., 2014 (PNAS meta-analysis of active learning)
- Prince, 2004 (active learning review)
- Mazur (Peer Instruction)
- Deslauriers et al., 2011
- Mayer (2001, 2009) multimedia learning
- Sweller (Cognitive Load Theory)

Given the argument rests on active learning and video pedagogy, these omissions are major.

---

## 12. Overreliance on Early 2010–2012 Sources  
**Severity: MEDIUM**

Most flipped classroom citations are early adopters. No:
- Meta-analyses
- Longitudinal studies
- Large-scale multi-institutional trials

The review lacks depth and maturity.

---

# NOVELTY POSITIONING

---

## 13. Limited Novel Contribution  
**Severity: MEDIUM**

The central idea seems to be:

> Combine flipped classroom + case studies + centralized video repository.

However:
- Case studies already involve pre-class preparation.
- Team-based learning already uses pre-class content.
- Blended learning already integrates online content.

The manuscript needs to clarify:
- What gap is being filled?
- What problem is unsolved by existing models?
- Why case-based flipping is uniquely powerful?

Currently, it reads as incremental rather than innovative.

---

---

## 14. Informal Tone  
**Severity: MEDIUM**

Examples:

> “Wait! Help is on the way…”  
> “Awesome Generation”  
> “buckle down and get serious…”

These rhetorical flourishes may suit a practitioner magazine but not a scholarly journal.

---

## 15. Uncritical Optimism  
**Severity: MEDIUM**

Statements such as:

> “All of this bodes well…”

signal advocacy rather than analysis.

A scholarly voice requires more measured conclusions.

---

# EQUITY AND ACCESS OMISSIONS

---

## 16. No Discussion of Access Inequality  
**Severity: MEDIUM**

Flipped models assume:

- Reliable internet  
- Device access  
- Quiet study spaces  

No discussion of:
- Socioeconomic disparities  
- Accessibility for students with disabilities  
- Captioning requirements  
- Bandwidth limitations  

This omission is notable.

---

# WHAT REVIEWER 2 WOULD SAY

---

> “The manuscript presents an enthusiastic advocacy for combining case studies with flipped instruction but lacks methodological rigor, theoretical grounding, and a clearly articulated scholarly contribution. The survey data are inadequately described, and the literature review selectively highlights positive findings while omitting major meta-analyses and theoretical frameworks. Substantial revision is required to clarify the manuscript’s purpose, strengthen the evidence base, and adopt a more critical and analytical tone.”

# RECOMMENDED REVISIONS

1. Clearly define manuscript type (empirical, review, perspective).
2. If empirical, fully report survey methodology and analysis.
3. Integrate seminal active learning and multimedia learning literature.
4. Differentiate flipped classroom from adjacent pedagogies.
5. Address equity and implementation barriers.
6. Temper claims; distinguish perception from measured learning.
7. Substantially proofread and correct typographical errors.
8. Provide a structured abstract.
9. Include critical evaluation of mixed or null findings.
10. Clarify the unique contribution of integrating cases with flipped delivery.

## Final Verdict (If Reviewed for a Research Journal)

**Major Revision Required**

Promising concept, but currently underdeveloped as a scholarly contribution and overly reliant on anecdotal and perception-based evidence.
